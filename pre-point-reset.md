# The Five-Second Pre-Point Reset

The easiest performance improvement to leave unused is the time between points. Technique
changes take practice, fitness changes take weeks, and equipment changes cost money. A
between-point reset can improve the next rally immediately.

The purpose is not to become perfectly calm. It is to stop the last point from choosing the
attention, tension, and intent of the next one.

## The routine

Use the same three steps after every rally, whether it ended with a winner, an error, or a
disagreement.

1. **Release.** Turn away from the net and exhale slowly. Let the paddle hang loosely for a
   moment. This marks the previous rally as finished and releases grip and shoulder tension.
2. **Choose.** Name one simple intention for the next point: the serve target, return
   target, third-shot shape, or player to pressure. Keep it external and observable.
3. **Set.** Face the court, take the ready position, and look at the ball. Once set, stop
   analyzing and play.

It should take about five seconds. A short cue such as "release, choose, set" makes the
sequence easier to remember under pressure.

## What counts as a useful intention

A useful intention directs attention without trying to control the whole rally:

- Serve deep to the backhand
- Return through the middle
- Give the third shot enough height to land short
- Keep the paddle in front during the first kitchen exchange
- Watch the ball through contact

"Do not miss," "play better," and "win this point" are not useful intentions. They describe
an outcome without giving attention a job.

## Doubles version

The reset should improve partnership rather than become a private ritual. Use one brief
communication during the choose step when needed:

- Confirm who takes the middle
- Agree on the return target
- Call a switch or stay
- Offer a quick paddle tap after a difficult rally

Do not use the gap to diagnose a partner's technique. Save adjustments that need a
conversation for the timeout or the end of the game.

## Practice progression

### Session 1: build the sequence

Use the routine after every point for one game. The only goal is to complete all three
steps. Do not judge whether it changes the score.

### Sessions 2 to 4: make the intention specific

Choose a target or ball shape before each point. Notice whether the intention is still
present when the ball arrives.

### Session 5 onward: use it when it matters

Keep the routine identical after consecutive errors, at game point, and when the pace
accelerates. Pressure is not the time to add steps. It is the reason the existing steps
were rehearsed.

## Score the habit

Track the routine for one game with a single tally:

| Measurement | Target |
|---|---|
| Points started after all three steps | 80% or more |

Do not track wins at first. Once the routine is automatic, compare stretches with and
without it using one performance measure such as unforced errors in the first three shots,
successful third-shot attempts, or returns made.

## Common failure modes

- **Only resetting after errors.** That turns the routine into a visible distress signal.
  Use it after every point so it stays neutral.
- **Turning the cue into instruction overload.** Choose one intention, not a list of swing
  mechanics.
- **Rushing after a mistake.** The urge to serve or receive immediately is often the last
  point leaking into the next one. Complete the exhale.
- **Dwelling on a winning rally.** Excitement carries tension too. Release good points as
  consistently as bad ones.
- **Changing the routine under pressure.** A routine becomes reliable through repetition,
  not complexity.

## The one-line version

Exhale and release the last rally, choose one external intention, then set and watch the
ball.
