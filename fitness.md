# Fitness for Pickleball

The sport hides how athletic it is. The court is small, so it feels forgiving, but the
movement pattern is short, sharp, repetitive, and almost entirely lateral. That combination
is exactly what untrained bodies are worst at. Most pickleball injuries are not contact
injuries. They are the result of asking a body for explosive change of direction that it was
never prepared for.

This page is about preparing for it.

## What the sport actually demands

Break a point down and the physical requirements are specific:

- **Repeated short accelerations and decelerations.** Two to four steps, stop, reset. The
  deceleration is the harder half and the one nobody trains.
- **Lateral movement and split steps.** Almost no straight-line running.
- **Low, sustained posture at the kitchen.** Dinking is an isometric hold in a half squat,
  which is why legs fatigue before lungs do.
- **Rotational power from the ground up.** Drives and overheads come from hips and trunk,
  not arms.
- **Reactive balance.** Reaching wide, recovering, and being ready before the next ball.
- **Repeatability across hours.** Open play means many short games with little rest, so
  aerobic base determines whether hour three looks like hour one.

## The four pillars

### 1. Mobility

The joints that need range: ankles, hips, thoracic spine, shoulders. Restriction in any of
them gets compensated for by the lower back or the knees.

- Ankle dorsiflexion drills (knee to wall)
- 90/90 hip rotations
- Thoracic rotations and open books
- Shoulder controlled articular rotations

Daily, ten minutes, more valuable than it looks.

### 2. Strength

Two or three sessions a week. The goal is resilient tissue and force production, not size.

- **Lower:** squat pattern, hinge pattern (deadlift or RDL), split squats or lunges
- **Upper:** horizontal push and pull, vertical push and pull
- **Trunk:** anti-rotation (Pallof press), anti-extension (dead bug, plank), loaded carries
- **Posterior shoulder:** external rotations and band pull-aparts, because the overhead is
  the shot most likely to hurt you

Single-leg work matters more than bilateral work here, since the sport is played on one leg
at a time.

### 3. Power and agility

Once a base exists, add speed. Small doses, full recovery, always before fatigue sets in.

- Lateral bounds and skater jumps
- Split-step drops into a shuffle
- Short cone patterns: side to side, up and back, first-step out of a split step
- Medicine ball rotational throws
- Deceleration drills specifically, meaning sprint three steps and stop under control

### 4. Conditioning

Match the energy system to the sport: mostly aerobic base with repeated short bursts on top.

- **Base:** two to three easy zone 2 sessions a week, thirty to forty five minutes
- **Specific:** intervals of ten to twenty seconds hard with forty to sixty seconds easy,
  which is roughly the work to rest ratio of a real rally

## A sample week

This assumes playing three days a week. Adjust down before you adjust up.

| Day | Focus |
|---|---|
| Mon | Strength (full body), mobility |
| Tue | Play, plus warm-up and cool-down |
| Wed | Zone 2 cardio, mobility |
| Thu | Play, plus short power and agility work beforehand |
| Fri | Strength (full body), mobility |
| Sat | Play, longer session |
| Sun | Rest or easy walk |

## Warm-up, non-negotiable

Five to ten minutes, before every session, before the first ball:

1. Two or three minutes easy movement to raise temperature
2. Dynamic mobility: leg swings, hip openers, trunk rotations
3. Progressive movement: shuffles, carioca, gradual acceleration
4. Split steps and a few short sprints at increasing effort
5. Easy dinking, then progressively harder shots

Cold starts into competitive play is where calf strains and Achilles injuries happen.

## Injury prevention, the specific ones

Pickleball has a short list of common injuries and most are preventable:

- **Achilles and calf.** Direct calf raises, both straight and bent knee, plus eccentric
  lowering. Warm up properly.
- **Lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow).** Usually a technique and grip pressure problem
  before it is a strength problem, but forearm eccentrics help. Loosen the grip.
- **Shoulder.** Rotator cuff and scapular strength, especially if you hit overheads.
- **Lower back.** Trunk anti-rotation strength and hip mobility. The back tends to pay for
  what the hips will not do.
- **Knees.** Deceleration strength and single-leg control.

## Recovery

Training is the stimulus, recovery is where adaptation happens.

- Sleep is the single largest lever. Everything else is a rounding error next to it.
- Protein adequate to bodyweight, spread across the day.
- Hydration, especially for outdoor play in heat.
- Deliberate rest days. Open play is seductive and easy to overdo, and most overuse injuries
  come from playing every day rather than training hard.

## Tracking

Worth keeping a simple log rather than a complicated one:

- Sessions played and rough duration
- Strength sessions completed and top loads
- Anything that hurt, when it started, and what preceded it
- Subjective energy, one to five

Patterns show up in weeks, not days. The log is what makes them visible.

---

*This is a general framework, not medical advice. Adjust for your own history, age, and
injuries, and see a professional for anything persistent.*
