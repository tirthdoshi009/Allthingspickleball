# Purpose at Pickleball

When I go to pickleball, I meet friends, but I do not always have a clear idea
of why I am there. I feel shy, and I find myself wondering what I can give
rather than only showing up to play.

My purpose can be simple: help someone feel welcome and have a good game.

I do not need to be the best player, coach people, or become unusually outgoing
to do that. A small contribution is still a contribution.

## Small Ways to Give

- Notice someone waiting alone and ask, "Do you want to join us?"
- Be a dependable partner: share the court and encourage them after a mistake.
- Ask whether a newcomer wants help with the rotation or score before explaining.
- Learn someone's name and greet them the next time.
- Do not give unsolicited advice. Helping does not have to mean teaching.

I do not need to sustain a long conversation for these actions to matter.

## Other Ways to Give

Giving can mean making the game easier to enjoy, not necessarily teaching or
helping someone improve.

- **Attention:** Learn names, remember something someone told me, and listen
  when I ask how their game went. I do not need to entertain them.
  Remembering our last conversation gives me somewhere to begin next time
  without having to invent something interesting to say.
- **Inclusion:** Invite someone waiting alone into a game, introduce people
  who have not met, or play with someone who gets overlooked. Ask rather than
  assume they need rescuing.
  "Do you want to join us?" is an invitation, not an obligation. They can
  decline, and I can leave the invitation there.
- **Steadiness:** Stay warm toward my partner after a mistake. "We're good --
  next point" lets them know they do not have to earn my approval on every shot.
  I can take the game seriously without making my partner carry my frustration.
  Being dependable includes how I behave when we are losing.
- **Fairness:** Make honest line calls, share court time, and acknowledge
  uncertainty rather than argue for every advantage.
  If I did not see a ball clearly, I can say so. Winning a disputed point is
  not more important than being someone others trust on the court.
- **Practical effort:** Pick up stray balls, help set up or put away equipment,
  or ask whether a newcomer wants help with the rotation or score.
  These are quiet ways to contribute. I can do a useful task without needing
  to lead the group or turn it into a conversation.
- **Companionship:** Sit with someone between games and be glad to see them
  without turning the interaction into advice.
  I can listen without trying to fix anything. The time between games can
  be part of why I show up, not merely a wait for the next match.
- **A good challenge:** Play attentively and take the other person seriously.
  Giving does not always mean going easy; a competitive game can be something
  we offer each other.
  I can give my effort while staying fair and friendly. Helping someone enjoy
  the game does not require giving away points or pretending not to care.

Attention and steadiness are places I can start without becoming the loudest
person on the court.
I can be someone people feel comfortable playing alongside.

## Respond to the Person

Giving is responding to a person, not finding a role that makes me
indispensable. I can offer something small, let them accept or decline it, and
go back to enjoying the game.

## One Intention for a Session

Today, I will make it easier for one person to belong here.

That is an intention, not a quota. I can notice what someone actually needs
rather than arrive with a job I must perform.

## I Can Receive, Too

Playing, enjoying my friends, and letting people get to know me are also valid
reasons to be there. I do not need to make myself useful to deserve a place on
the court.

Purpose can guide how I participate without becoming another performance
requirement.
